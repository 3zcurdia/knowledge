# AI Datacenter Debt Hidden Off Balance Sheet

## The Hyperion loophole

Meta's Hyperion data center in rural Louisiana — big enough that Zuckerberg posted its outline laid over a map of Manhattan, covering a big chunk of the island — needed $27 billion to build. Borrowing that $27 billion itself would have tanked Meta's stock, so Meta engineered a loophole: it let investment firm Blue Owl own the majority of the project and then agreed to rent it back. The debt stays off Meta's balance sheet, hidden from investors, while Wall Street unloads the risky bonds onto public pension funds. PIMCO scooped up about $18 billion of the Hyperion bonds using the retirement money of millions. Those bonds run until 2049, but Meta's first lease on the building lasts only four years — and after that Mark Zuckerberg can just walk away, leaving ordinary Americans holding the bag.

## The scale of hidden promises

Across the biggest spenders, Burry counts upward of $3 trillion in promises like this — more than Canada's entire economy produces in a year. And the borrowing they cannot hide is exploding: the industry giants are burning through cash so fast that lenders are demanding over 7% interest just to hand it over.

## Nvidia banks its own customers

Nvidia has already sunk $30 billion into OpenAI and guaranteed up to $105 billion of the company's data center leases — if OpenAI defaults, Nvidia pays every cent — to a company that was hemorrhaging money, losing $3 for every dollar it brought in in 2025. Nvidia also owns a $4.7 billion stake in chip rental company CoreWeave and buys back whatever computing power CoreWeave cannot sell, sitting on both sides of the table. Nvidia now has well over $200 billion tied up in its own customers, much of it circling straight back as chip orders that Wall Street counts as growth.

## Banks, insurers, and pension funds pick up the tab

Oracle founder Larry Ellison borrowed tens of billions to build OpenAI's data centers; the reckless bet briefly made him the richest man alive, and now half of Oracle's future orders hang on a company that has never turned a profit. Oracle and OpenAI abruptly pulled the plug on a planned expansion in Abilene, and S&P cut Oracle's credit rating to one step above junk while banks sit on billions in toxic Oracle loans they cannot sell. To raise the $800 billion needed to fund the next set of data centers, Wall Street went hunting for cash and found it in insurance funds: firms like Apollo and KKR bought up entire insurers — the ones that sell the annuities retirees count on — and since 2017 buyout-owned insurers have quadrupled the money going into data centers. Private credit defaults have already hit the highest rate on record; at Blue Owl, investors tried to pull almost 40% of their tech lending fund two quarters in a row before it was shut down. Nvidia has teamed up with Apollo, KKR, and four other Wall Street firms to raise more than $500 billion for its own customers, most of it drained from insurance and retirement funds.
