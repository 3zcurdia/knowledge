# Moore's law

Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit (IC) doubles about every two years, with minimal increase in cost. Despite the name, it describes an empirical relationship, not a scientific law.

Named after Gordon Moore, co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel, who in 1965 noted that the number of components per integrated circuit had been doubling every year. In 1975, he revised the forecast to doubling every two years — a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 41%.

## Key metrics

- **Transistor doubling**: every ~2 years (originally every year, revised in 1975)
- **CAGR**: 41%
- **David House prediction**: computer chip performance doubles every 18 months (combining Moore's law with Dennard scaling)
- **As of 2025**: commercially available processors reach 92.2+ billion transistors (e.g., GB202 GPU)

## Related laws

- **Dennard scaling** (1974): as MOS transistors get smaller, power density stays constant. Ended in mid-2000s due to leakage currents.
- **Moore's second law / Rock's law**: capital cost of a semiconductor fabrication plant doubles every 4 years.
- **Kryder's law**: hard disk drive areal density doubling (over-optimistic; slowed ~2010).
- **Keck's law**: fiber-optic capacity increases faster than Moore's law.
- **Eroom's law**: cost of developing a new drug doubles every 9 years (Moore's Law backward).

## Current status

Semiconductor advancement has slowed industry-wide since ~2010. Intel's Pat Gelsinger (2023): "we're probably doubling effectively closer to every three years now." Nvidia's Jensen Huang declared Moore's law dead in 2022; Intel countered it's not dead but harder.

The ITRS produced its final roadmap in 2016, shifting from Moore's law scaling to a "More than Moore" strategy driven by application needs.
