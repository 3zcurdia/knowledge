# TPU vs GPU Microarchitecture Trade-offs

## Designing for one job

In the 2010s training required high-precision 32-bit floating point — exactly what GPUs were built for — but inference is sped up by compressing or quantizing a trained model to lower precision, and at the time GPUs did not support integer operations; they also carried a lot of general-purpose machinery that was overkill for model inference. Jeff Dean did the analysis and his conclusion was, in effect: given what Google was going to spend on this in capacity, we'll just do an ASIC instead. An ASIC — an application-specific integrated circuit — is a chip designed for a very narrow task: in this case running neural networks and nothing else. The first TPU was optimized for 8-bit integer operations, so its MAC units were tiny; at its peak it executed 92 trillion operations per second, about 35 times more than the CPU running in Google servers at the time.

## Floor plans tell the story

When you design a processor, the trade-offs are always between memory bandwidth — memory speed — how close or far the memory is from the processing units, and the size of the memory. The TPU floor plan shows where Google spent its silicon: a quarter of it goes to the matrix multiply unit (the systolic array), the buffer that feeds it with activations takes up another third of the surface, and the control logic — the part that does a lot of predictive work in a regular CPU — covers only 2%. Compare the floor plan of Nvidia's K80 die: it is tiled with over a dozen identical blocks called streaming multiprocessors, which share a large cache in the middle, and each streaming multiprocessor has its own instruction cache, warp schedulers, and register files. A significant portion of the die is dedicated to things that are simply not needed for model inference. The TPU removed all of it: caches, branch prediction, out-of-order execution, multiprocessing, speculative prefetching. The same minimalism carried into the instruction set: while Intel's x86 has about a thousand instructions, the first TPU was implemented by basic operations like read weights or read host memory for the model inputs. The TPU does not even fetch these instructions itself — it depends on an external host CPU connected through PCIe that sends them to a local instruction buffer. In Google's own words, the first TPU was closer to a floating-point co-processor than it was to a GPU.

## Results and the tensor-core difference

It turned out to be a very practical strategy: the first TPU was 15 times faster than Nvidia's K80 at less than half the power, though Nvidia would shrink that gap over the next few years. In the meantime Nvidia developed its own line of machine-learning GPUs, starting in 2017 with the Volta architecture and including the well-known Hopper, Blackwell, and now Rubin. What differentiates them from gaming GPUs is an on-chip component called the tensor core. Nvidia has not disclosed the exact mechanism, but there is a meaningful difference: while a TPU multiplies two big matrix tiles at once, a GPU has many tensor cores, each multiplying two very small tiles. This decomposition gives more power to the scheduler — at runtime it can decide which thread runs next, which includes which block of matrix lanes gets multiplied next. Beyond that, Google and Nvidia have been pulling similar levers.

## Memory: the lever both sides pull

One lever is memory bandwidth: between TPU V1 and V8 it grew 200x, with the single biggest jump in V2, when DRAM chips soldered across the circuit board were replaced with high-bandwidth memory (HBM). HBM is still DRAM, but the dies are stacked vertically; both the HBM and the processor sit on the same slab of silicon with thousands of microscopic wires etched into it — a shorter distance with more wires, hence the high bandwidth. Nvidia had made the switch to HBM even before Volta, and because HBM is made by third-party manufacturers, both sides get the improvements for free — which is also why a TPU and a GPU from the same year end up with roughly the same bandwidth. Alongside memory, peak compute grew as well: between TPU V2 and V7, at 16-bit precision, it went up 50 times — mostly the usual story of smaller transistors, more of them, and higher clock rates. Scaling out does not change the memory bottleneck: each individual device still struggles with it. Google now places four or even eight TPU chips on the same board and connects thousands of boards into what they call a pod — V7 can put together more than 9,000 TPUs — while Nvidia does the same with NVLink, 72 GPUs wired into a single rack. In 2023 DeepMind served a quantized Llama 3.1 70B model on a TPU v5e with a batch size of 120 to keep the TPU busy, and the math works out very similarly even for the newest TPUs and GPUs, since they still rely on HBM.
